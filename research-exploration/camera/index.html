CASE FILE 01 · THE MISSING FRAME

FRAME

THE EVIDENCE SPEAKS

Three interviews. Repeated behaviours. A pattern worth investigating.

10

WITNESS 01 · PRAJAKTA, 26

Her friends often failed to take photographs the way she wanted. Her workaround: turn the phone towards herself, set the pose using the live preview, then ask her friend to simply hold the phone and tap the shutter.

Her idea: set the pose and framing first, then hand the phone to another person.

WHAT IT REVEALED

She was handing the framing and directing back to herself because the photographer could not reliably execute her intention.

WITNESS 02 · SACHIN

He handed his phone to his sister, who struggled to frame him. The background took up too much of the photograph while his full figure appeared too small. He also needed around 15 attempts to get a satisfactory selfie.

WHAT IT REVEALED

The dyad problem is not only about posing. It also involves translating the intended composition into the correct camera framing.

● REC · f/2.8 · 1/125 · ISO 400

CASE FILE 01 · THE MISSING FRAME

FRAME

THE EVIDENCE SPEAKS

Witness 03, and what the three interviews add up to.

10

WITNESS 03 · CHRIS, 25

On a trip to Meghalaya, his friends guided him on how to pose and whether he looked confident. He said the guidance worked. He also described discomfort when attention was drawn towards him in group photographs.

WHAT IT REVEALED

Real-time guidance from another person can change the experience of being photographed. The human guide performed a role the camera did not provide.

CROSS-INTERVIEW INTERPRETATION

The interviews support the broader capture-guidance problem. Prajakta and Sachin provide two distinct dyad-related data points. Chris shows the value of real-time human guidance, particularly for confidence.

These findings suggest an opportunity: investigate how a camera can support coordination rather than merely record the final image.

CASE FILE 01 · THE MISSING FRAME

FRAME

THE NEW PROBLEM STATEMENT

The problem is not simply taking a photograph. It is translating an intention into a photograph.

12

When a person asks someone else to take their photograph, the outcome depends on coordination between the subject and the photographer. Without a shared visual reference and timely feedback, both rely on repeated verbal corrections and trial-and-error captures.

FOCUSED PROBLEM STATEMENT

People struggle to achieve their intended photographs when a non-expert photographer is involved, because the subject’s visual intention is not effectively translated into actionable framing, positioning and capture decisions.

GAPS A DESIGNER CAN WORK ON

Give the subject a way to communicate. Give the photographer a way to execute.

14

OPEN CHALLENGE

Explore an experience that bridges the communication and execution gap between a subject and a non-expert photographer.

OPPORTUNITY AREAS · HOW MIGHT WE

  • Capture or establish the subject’s intended pose and framing.
  • Communicate that intention through a clear visual reference.
  • Help the photographer understand the required camera position, angle and composition.
  • Provide timely feedback before the shutter is pressed.
  • Keep both people involved without increasing the social awkwardness of being photographed.
