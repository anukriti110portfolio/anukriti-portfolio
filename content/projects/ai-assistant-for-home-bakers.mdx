<Section title="Problem statement">

<Lead>Small business owners who take orders on WhatsApp lose orders to late or missed replies, but are afraid that an AI replying in their name could promise what their kitchen cannot deliver.</Lead>

How might we enable a small business owner to let AI manage customer conversations, without losing control of her business or her customers' trust?

</Section>

<Section title="Stakes">

<Lead>A helpful-sounding reply can cost her a cake, a customer or a Saturday.</Lead>

The owner runs a home bakery from her kitchen. Nearly every order arrives on WhatsApp, at night, during school pickup, or while she is mid-bake. Some messages go unanswered, some get late replies, and orders are lost. An assistant could fix that. She sees the value, and she hesitates, because her name is on every reply.

<Facts
  cols={2}
  items={[
    { title: "Without help", bullets: ["Chats go unanswered while her hands are busy.", "Late replies lose the order to another baker.", "She is sales, support and kitchen from one phone."] },
    { title: "With a generic assistant", bullets: ["It fills in a complete-looking order and says yes to a Saturday that already has two cakes.", "It promises a photo cake by morning she has no time or supplies for.", "It offers a refund she never approved."] },
  ]}
/>

The question I was given: how might we enable a small business owner to let AI manage customer conversations, without losing control of her business or her customers' trust? Most AI tools are built for the customer's side of the chat. This case is about the owner's side, including what happens when the assistant is wrong.

</Section>

<Section title="Insight">

<Lead>Completeness is not capability.</Lead>

Both bakers had refused an order after every detail was filled in: an intricate small bento cake, and 2 kg detail squeezed onto 0.5 kg. The form was complete. The cake was not something they could stand behind.

So the trust risk is not that an assistant might chat. It is that a reply can commit her kitchen and her name, in a tone that sounds finished. The line to design is between answering and promising.

</Section>

<Section title="User flows">

<Lead>Four scenarios, one rule: the owner steps in only where a promise begins.</Lead>

I split the work into four flows. The first three cover how an order gets from enquiry to confirmed. The fourth covers what happens after.

### 1 · Standard order, no custom design

Goal: the customer explores, picks and confirms on WhatsApp without needing the owner. Prototype: Priya (works) and Aarav (doesn't).

<Steps
  items={[
    { title: "Customer", text: "Opens WhatsApp and asks about the bakery, kitchen hygiene and prices." },
    { title: "Assistant", text: "Shares her story, from-scratch approach, a kitchen photo, designs with price per kg. It says it is the assistant." },
    { title: "Customer", text: "Picks a listed design and a simple customisation, such as a name on the cake." },
    { title: "Assistant", text: "Collects weight, pickup or delivery, time. Summarises with \"not started baking, reply yes to confirm\"." },
    { title: "If the options work", text: "Customer replies yes. The assistant forwards the order to the owner. Kitchen starts only after she checks the calendar." },
    { title: "If the options don't work", text: "The assistant sends her waiting line and does not promise the design. The owner takes the chat when the customer dislikes the list, needs an unlisted option, or the request needs her judgement on feasibility." },
  ]}
/>

### 2 · Urgent order

Goal: quick orders go through fast, and the owner is clearly alerted when one is confirmed. Prototype: Rahul.

<Steps
  items={[
    { title: "Assistant", text: "States the advance-order requirement first: please order at least 5 hours ahead. Shows only designs eligible for urgent orders." },
    { title: "Path A · picks an available cake", text: "The customer chooses a pre-approved design and enters details. The assistant checks the urgent-order conditions and never promises availability on its own. The owner gets a distinct ringing alert with the order details once the urgent order is confirmed." },
    { title: "Path B · wants something different", text: "The assistant connects the customer to the owner to check feasibility. She decides if the cake can be prepared and delivered in the customer's time. Then the assistant can confirm." },
  ]}
/>

A customer turned away on an urgent request assumes quick orders are not possible and does not come back, so the rule is shown up front. The alarm fires only on a confirmed urgent order, not on every enquiry. A normal WhatsApp notification may not ring reliably, so this needs its own notification with permission. The 5-hour window is Megha's rule. The owner sets her own.

### 3 · Custom cake through consultation

Goal: understand the vision, document what was agreed, get an explicit yes. An enquiry is not an order. Prototype: Ravi (draft she approves).

<Steps
  items={[
    { title: "Customer", text: "Wants a design or customisation the list does not offer." },
    { title: "Assistant", text: "Connects them to the owner." },
    { title: "The owner", text: "Discusses design, occasion and expectations. Records price, date and details." },
    { title: "Assistant", text: "Writes the agreed specification as an order summary in the chat, so both sides have the same record." },
    { title: "The owner", text: "Reconfirms design, price, delivery schedule and terms. The order is ready to bake only now." },
    { title: "Payment", text: "First-time customer: the assistant asks for the owner's advance of ₹100–₹200 and helps with payment, marking it received only after the transaction is verified. Existing customer: The owner follows her usual payment policy, with design, status, date and payment recorded in one place." },
  ]}
/>

### 4 · After the order: delivery, feedback, review

Goal: the assistant watches the loose ends so the owner does not carry them in her head. Designed, not in the prototype.

<Steps
  items={[
    { title: "Log", text: "The assistant logs the confirmed order and assigns a slot first-come, first-served." },
    { title: "Watch", text: "It watches the delivery window and payment. If a delivery runs late or a payment confirmation is missing, it sends the owner an urgent alert card." },
    { title: "Feedback", text: "When she marks the order delivered, it sends a short feedback form on WhatsApp and routes the reply to her." },
    { title: "Review", text: "At month end, it compiles orders, sales and feedback into a plain list of pros and cons." },
  ]}
/>

<Section title="Decisions">

<Lead>Four forks that shaped the product.</Lead> Each one had a reasonable alternative. Here is what I weighed and what I dropped.

### When should the owner be in the loop: before, during or after the assistant speaks?

<Facts
  cols={3}
  items={[
    { title: "A · Permission sheet (used in part)", text: "She sets rules once, up front. Feels like homework at 1–2 enquiries a week, and fails if a custom request looks complete." },
    { title: "B · Needs you list (shipped)", text: "Silence on routine, a clear ask on judgement. Built for hands-in-batter hours. Gets noisy if the list is wrong." },
    { title: "C · Recoverable send (used in part)", text: "Contains damage after a wrong promise. Interrupts too late to be the whole product." },
  ]}
/>

I shipped B for daily use, with A's defaults and C's repair, because CrumbLust asked for "a combination": independent on basics, her on custom designs and final confirmations. She did not want to approve every reply.

### What should an angry customer receive first?

<Facts
  cols={3}
  items={[
    { title: "A · Silence, then page the owner (killed)", text: "Nothing canned reaches a hurt customer. It was my research bet. She did not ask for it." },
    { title: "B · Her apology line and one question (shipped)", text: "Acknowledges them immediately, in her words, and commits to nothing. Might still sound like a bot." },
    { title: "C · Assistant offers a fix (killed)", text: "A refund is a commitment only she can make. Blaming the rider before she knows is a guess." },
  ]}
/>

I dropped my own silence idea. She wrote the apology and waiting line herself and ruled out any refund or resolution without her. The risk that a canned sorry feels like not being listened to is still open, and it is the third thing I would test.

### What counts as a yes?

<Facts
  cols={3}
  items={[
    { title: "A · All details filled equals order (killed)", text: "Fastest for the customer, but both bakers refused complete orders they could not do." },
    { title: "B · The owner says yes equals order (not enough)", text: "Her judgement is in, but Megha once baked a negotiated order the client then dropped." },
    { title: "C · Her yes, then the customer confirms back (shipped)", text: "Matches Megha's double confirmation. The draft says \"I won't bake until you confirm\"." },
  ]}
/>

Kitchen work starts after two yeses. This protects her ingredients and her afternoon, and the prototype states it in the details panel of every chat.

### Where does the assistant live?

<Facts
  cols={2}
  items={[
    { title: "A · Customer chatbot as the product (killed)", text: "The brief is about the owner. Megha rejects intake bots." },
    { title: "B · Web dashboard (killed)", text: "She runs everything from one phone, often with floury hands." },
    { title: "C · Companion phone layer (first pass)", text: "Shows \"not promised\" without rereading, but is a second surface she may never open." },
    { title: "D · Inside her WhatsApp Business inbox (shipped)", text: "Status chips, filters, approvals and Take over sit where she already works. Real WhatsApp behaviour is mocked." },
  ]}
/>

I first built nine screens as a companion layer, then moved the same ideas into the inbox itself. Customers and Megha already avoid extra apps. The owner should not need a new one either.
